# Time tracking in Spain: what the law requires, and what you are being sold

<div className="mt-6 border-l-2 border-brand pl-5 text-[18px] leading-relaxed text-fg">
  Recording working hours has been mandatory since 2019 and plenty of companies still do not know
  it. Doing it digitally is **not** mandatory, and plenty believe it is because someone selling
  something told them so. Here is the difference, and what actually changes when your team works
  out on the road.
</div>

## What has been mandatory since 2019

Article 34.9 of the Workers' Statute, as worded by Royal Decree-Law 8/2019, requires every company
to keep **a daily record of the working day** of each employee, with its start and end time. It has
been in force since 12 May 2019.

There is no headcount threshold. A company of three is as bound as one of three hundred. Nor does it
depend on the sector, or on whether people work in an office or move from site to site.

Records have to be **kept for four years** and remain available to the employees, to their legal
representatives and to the Labour Inspectorate.

## What is NOT required, however often you are told otherwise

This is where care pays off, because a lot gets sold on the back of it.

**The law does not require digital clocking in.** It requires a record; it does not impose the
means. A signed paper record that holds what the rule asks for and is kept for four years is legal
today.

A regulation has indeed been drafted to require records to be digital and remotely accessible to
the Inspectorate. That draft received an **unfavourable opinion from the Council of State** and has
not been published in the official gazette.

<Destacado titulo="How to spot the sales argument">
  If somebody tells you that "clocking in through an app is already compulsory", or that "you will
  be fined without a digital system", they are presenting as law something that is not.

  That does not make paper a good idea. It means **the reason to change is not the fine**: it is
  that paper does not do the job you need it to do. And that is a different conversation, one where
  you can choose calmly instead of buying out of fear.
</Destacado>

## Why paper breaks in a service company

In an office with fixed hours, a signed sheet works reasonably well. In a maintenance, cleaning,
landscaping or HVAC company in Mallorca, where people start in one place and finish in another, it
breaks in several places at once.

The underlying problem is not legal: it is that the record is no use for anything else. It is paper
kept for four years in case the Inspectorate calls, and until then it informs not one decision.

## What changes when clocking in lives where the work is

If clocking in lives on the phone the technician already carries, it stops being paperwork and
starts answering questions the office asks every day.

- **The time is the real one.** Clocked at the start, on site, not from Friday's memory.
- **The office sees who is on the clock.** And can tell a customer somebody is on the way without
  calling four phones.
- **Hours add themselves up.** Payroll no longer means working through a stack of paper.
- **And the record is kept.** All four years, without a folder somebody has to guard.

<Nota>
  Location can be recorded at the moment of clocking in if it is proportionate and clearly
  disclosed. What is not acceptable is tracking the person throughout the working day. The line runs
  exactly between a single point and a permanent trail, and it belongs in writing in the notice given
  to staff, not merely understood.
</Nota>

## The change that actually makes it pay

Now the part almost nobody mentions, and the one that separates clocking in from clocking in that
is worth having.

If the technician clocks in on one app and then records the work done somewhere else, you have
added one more chore to their day. Two apps, two moments of typing, and the second gets filled in
badly because it is seven in the evening.

If clocking in and the <Link href="/modulos/partes-de-trabajo">job report</Link> are the same thing,
the arithmetic reverses: the technician opens the phone once per job, and out of it come the hours,
the work done, the materials and the customer's signature. One gesture instead of three, and the
office learns all of it at once.
